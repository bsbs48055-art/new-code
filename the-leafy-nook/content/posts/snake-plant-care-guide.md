If there's one houseplant that deserves its reputation for being nearly indestructible, it's the snake plant (*Dracaena trifasciata*, formerly classified as *Sansevieria*). It tolerates low light, irregular watering, dry indoor air, and the kind of benign neglect that kills most other houseplants. The one thing it genuinely can't tolerate is overwatering — which, ironically, is the most common way people accidentally kill it.

## Light requirements

Snake plants are remarkably adaptable, tolerating everything from low, indirect light to bright, direct sun.

- **Ideal:** Bright, indirect light, which produces the fastest, most vigorous growth.
- **Tolerated well:** Low light (see our [low-light plant guide](/blog/best-low-light-houseplants-for-dark-apartments/)), though growth will be noticeably slower.
- **Also tolerated:** Several hours of direct sun, though intense afternoon sun in a hot climate can occasionally scorch leaves.

There are very few lighting conditions in an average home that will seriously harm a snake plant — this flexibility is a major reason it's recommended so often for beginners.

## Watering (the part that actually matters)

This is where snake plants get killed, almost always through overwatering rather than underwatering.

- Water only when the soil is **completely dry** at least 2–3 inches down — for most homes, this works out to roughly **every 2–4 weeks**, and even less often in winter.
- When you do water, water thoroughly until it runs from the drainage holes, then let it dry out fully again before the next watering.
- Never let the pot sit in standing water in a saucer.

If you're unsure whether to water, wait a few more days — snake plants store water in their thick, upright leaves and rhizomes, making them far more tolerant of a delayed watering than an early one.

## Soil and pot

Use a fast-draining mix — a cactus/succulent blend, or a standard potting mix amended with extra perlite (roughly 30–40% perlite by volume), works well. A pot with drainage holes is essential; see our [pot sizing guide](/blog/how-to-choose-the-right-pot-size/) for choosing the right size, and don't size up more than necessary, since snake plants actually do well slightly root-bound.

## Fertilizing

Snake plants are light feeders. A balanced liquid fertilizer diluted to half strength, applied once every 6–8 weeks during spring and summer, is plenty. Skip fertilizing entirely in fall and winter.

## Common problems and what they actually mean

| Symptom | Likely cause |
| --- | --- |
| Mushy, yellowing leaves at the base | Overwatering — the single most common issue. Reduce watering frequency immediately and check roots for rot. |
| Leaves splitting or curling | Usually underwatering, though far less common than overwatering-related issues for this species. |
| Brown tips | Often low humidity or a buildup of fertilizer salts/fluoride from tap water — try filtered or distilled water if this persists. |
| Slow or no new growth | Often just normal — snake plants are naturally slow growers, especially in lower light. Not usually a sign of a problem on its own. |
| Leaves falling over/flopping | Can indicate overwatering (softened base) or insufficient light causing weak growth; check firmness at the base first. |

## Propagation

Snake plants can be propagated two ways:

1. **Division:** When repotting, separate the rhizome (the thick horizontal root structure) into sections, each with at least one healthy leaf fan and some roots attached, and pot each section separately. This is the fastest, most reliable method and produces plants identical to the parent.
2. **Leaf cuttings:** Cut a healthy leaf into 2–3 inch sections, let the cut ends callus for a day, then insert the bottom edge into slightly moist, well-draining soil. This works but is slower, and cuttings from variegated (striped yellow-edge) varieties often revert to solid green, since the variegation isn't always genetically stable through this method.

## Is a snake plant safe for pets?

Snake plants are mildly toxic to cats and dogs if chewed, typically causing gastrointestinal upset rather than severe poisoning. It's still worth keeping out of reach of pets prone to chewing plants — see our [full pet-safe plant guide](/blog/pet-safe-houseplants-guide/) for genuinely non-toxic alternatives if that's a priority in your home.

## Why snake plants are such a good starter plant

Snake plants tolerate the two most common beginner mistakes — inconsistent watering schedules and imperfect light — better than almost any other houseplant, while still looking architectural and attractive rather than "beginner." If you're building confidence with houseplants before moving on to fussier species, this is one of the best places to start.
