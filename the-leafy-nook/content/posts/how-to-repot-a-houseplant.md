Repotting looks simple — move plant, add soil, done — but a surprising number of houseplant setbacks happen right after a repot, usually from sizing up too aggressively, damaging roots carelessly, or repotting at the wrong time. Here's how to do it in a way that minimizes stress on the plant.

## When to repot

- Roots are visibly circling the top of the soil or growing out of the drainage holes.
- The plant needs watering unusually often because there's more root than soil to hold moisture.
- Growth has stalled despite otherwise good light and care.
- It's been 2+ years since the last repot, even without other obvious signs — soil breaks down and loses structure over time.
- You're dealing with root rot from overwatering (see our [overwatering guide](/blog/signs-of-overwatering-houseplants/)) and need to remove damaged roots and refresh the soil.

## When *not* to repot

- The plant is already showing stress (wilting, dropping leaves) from an unrelated cause — fix that issue first, since repotting adds its own stress on top.
- It's the middle of winter, when the plant is dormant and slow to recover from any root disturbance. Wait for spring if the situation isn't urgent.
- The plant was just recently repotted (within the last few months) — give it time to establish before repotting again, even if you're tempted to "fix" something.

## What you'll need

- A new pot only 1–2 inches larger in diameter than the current one (see our [pot sizing guide](/blog/how-to-choose-the-right-pot-size/) for exact sizing), with drainage holes.
- Fresh potting mix suited to the plant type (see our [soil guide](/blog/indoor-plant-soil-guide-for-beginners/)).
- Clean, sharp scissors or shears for trimming any damaged roots.
- A work surface you don't mind getting a little messy — a tarp, old towel, or doing this outdoors/on a balcony helps.

## Step-by-step process

1. **Water the plant a day or two before repotting.** A well-hydrated root ball holds together better and is less likely to fall apart or tear during the process, compared to bone-dry soil.
2. **Prepare the new pot.** Add a shallow layer of fresh soil at the bottom — enough that when the plant is placed in, its soil line sits about half an inch below the pot's rim.
3. **Remove the plant from its current pot.** Turn it on its side, gently squeeze a flexible plastic pot to loosen the root ball, or run a thin knife around the inside edge of a rigid pot before easing the plant out. Avoid pulling on the stem itself.
4. **Inspect the roots.** Firm, pale roots are healthy. Trim away any brown, black, or mushy roots with clean scissors, cutting back to firm tissue. If roots are densely circling the root ball ("root-bound"), gently loosen the outer layer with your fingers to encourage them to grow outward into the new soil rather than continuing to circle.
5. **Place the plant in the new pot** on top of the soil layer you added, checking that the top of the root ball sits at the right height (roughly half an inch below the rim, same depth it was growing at before).
6. **Fill in around the sides with fresh soil**, gently firming it down to eliminate large air pockets, but without compacting it so tightly that drainage suffers.
7. **Water thoroughly** until it runs from the drainage holes — this also helps the soil settle naturally around the roots.
8. **Skip fertilizer for 4–6 weeks.** Freshly disturbed roots can't process nutrients efficiently right away, and added fertilizer salts add unnecessary stress during recovery.

## Minimizing transplant shock

- Keep the plant in the same light conditions it was in before repotting, at least for the first couple of weeks — don't repot and relocate to a very different light level at the same time.
- Expect some temporary drooping or minor leaf drop in the days after repotting; this is normal and usually resolves within 1–2 weeks as roots settle into the new soil.
- Avoid repotting and pruning heavily at the same time unless you're specifically dealing with root rot — recovering from both stresses simultaneously is harder on the plant.
- Keep humidity slightly elevated if convenient (a pebble tray nearby, for instance) for the first week or two, especially for humidity-sensitive plants.

## Top-dressing: an alternative for plants already in their max practical pot size

For large floor plants where sizing up further isn't practical (a 16-inch pot is heavy and takes up significant space), you can "top-dress" instead: scrape away the top 1–2 inches of old soil without disturbing the root ball, and replace it with fresh soil mixed with a little compost or slow-release fertilizer. This refreshes nutrients without the disruption of a full repot, and is a reasonable approach every year or two for plants that have reached their practical size limit.
