"Water once a week" is probably the most repeated — and most misleading — piece of houseplant advice out there. It ignores your pot size, your plant species, your home's humidity, the season, and how much light the plant is actually getting. Two identical pothos plants in two different apartments can have completely different watering needs.

Instead of a calendar, use your plant's actual soil as the signal.

## The finger test (free and works every time)

Stick your index finger into the soil up to the second knuckle (about 2 inches / 5 cm deep).

- **Feels wet or cool and slightly sticks to your finger** → wait, don't water yet.
- **Feels dry and crumbly** → time to water most tropical houseplants.
- **Feels dry more than halfway down the whole pot** → for moisture-loving plants like ferns or calatheas, this is already a bit later than ideal.

This one habit prevents the single most common houseplant killer: watering on a schedule instead of watering based on need.

## The pot-weight trick

Once you've watered a plant thoroughly and felt how heavy the pot is, lift it again a few days later. A pot that's dried out feels noticeably lighter than one that's still holding water. After doing this a few times, you'll be able to tell a plant needs water just by picking it up — no soil check required. This works especially well for plastic nursery pots, which are light enough to feel the difference clearly.

## General watering frequency by plant type

These are starting points, not rules — always confirm with the finger test:

| Plant type | Typical frequency (avg. home) | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Snake plant, ZZ plant | Every 3–4 weeks | Let soil dry out completely between waterings |
| Pothos, philodendron | Every 7–10 days | Water when top 2 inches are dry |
| Peace lily | Every 5–7 days | Will droop dramatically when thirsty — a helpful warning sign |
| Ferns, calathea, prayer plant | Every 4–6 days | Prefer consistently moist (not soggy) soil |
| Succulents, cacti | Every 2–3 weeks | Soak fully, then let dry completely |
| Monstera, philodendron (large) | Every 7–12 days | Adjust based on pot size and light |

## Signs your plant is thirsty

- Leaves feel slightly soft or look dull rather than glossy.
- Soil has pulled away from the sides of the pot.
- Lower/older leaves yellow and drop, but the plant otherwise looks fine (this can also mean overwatering — check soil moisture to tell them apart).
- Peace lilies, in particular, will visibly wilt.

## Signs you're watering too often

Overwatering is responsible for far more houseplant deaths than underwatering, because it's less obvious in the moment.

- Yellowing leaves that feel soft or mushy rather than crispy.
- Soil that stays wet for more than a week after watering.
- A musty or sour smell coming from the pot.
- Brown, mushy patches near the base of the stem.
- Fungus gnats hovering around the soil (they breed in consistently damp soil).

We break down overwatering in much more detail, including exactly how to fix it, in [our guide to spotting and reversing overwatering](/blog/signs-of-overwatering-houseplants/).

## Does the season matter?

Yes, significantly. Most houseplants slow their growth in fall and winter as daylight hours drop, even indoors under artificial light. That means they use less water. It's common (and correct) to water a plant every week in July and only every 2–3 weeks in January for that exact same plant. Reduce your watering frequency as the days shorten, and increase it again in spring as new growth appears.

## How to actually water (technique matters too)

1. Water until it runs freely out of the drainage holes — this ensures the whole root ball gets moisture, not just the top layer.
2. Let the pot drain completely; never let it sit in standing water in a saucer or cachepot.
3. Use room-temperature water when possible — cold water can shock tropical plant roots.
4. If your tap water is very hard or heavily chlorinated, letting it sit out for 24 hours before use helps some sensitive plants (like calathea) avoid leaf-tip browning.

## A simple rule to remember

When in doubt, wait a day and check again. Underwatering is uncomfortable for a plant but almost always recoverable. Overwatering damages roots silently and is the harder mistake to reverse — so if you're unsure, err on the side of a little more patience before you reach for the watering can.
