Pothos (*Epipremnum aureum*) shows up on nearly every "easiest houseplants" list for good reason: it tolerates a wide range of light, forgives inconsistent watering, grows quickly, and roots from cuttings almost effortlessly. If you're building confidence with houseplants, this is one of the best places to start — and even experienced growers keep pothos around for how reliably good it looks with minimal effort.

## Light requirements

- **Ideal:** Bright, indirect light produces the fastest growth and, for variegated varieties, the most distinct leaf patterning.
- **Tolerated well:** Low to medium indirect light (solid green varieties handle this better than variegated ones, which may revert to solid green over time without enough light).
- **Avoid:** Intense, direct afternoon sun, which can scorch leaves, especially those with lighter variegated patches that have less chlorophyll to protect them.

## Watering

Pothos wants to dry out noticeably between waterings — this is a plant that's much more likely to suffer from overwatering than underwatering.

- Water when the top 1–2 inches of soil are dry, typically every **7–10 days**, though this varies with light and season.
- Drooping, slightly softened leaves are pothos's way of signaling thirst — a helpful, low-stakes warning sign, since it perks back up quickly once watered.
- Consistently yellow, soft leaves (especially several at once) point to overwatering rather than thirst — see our [overwatering guide](/blog/signs-of-overwatering-houseplants/).

## Soil and pot

A standard, well-draining indoor potting mix works well; adding 10–20% perlite improves drainage further if your mix tends to stay wet for a long time after watering. A pot with drainage holes is important, and pothos is happy to be slightly root-bound, so don't size up more than the 1–2 inch rule described in our [pot sizing guide](/blog/how-to-choose-the-right-pot-size/).

## Fertilizing

Feed with a balanced liquid fertilizer diluted to half strength every 3–4 weeks during spring and summer. Pothos is a light feeder and grows well even with minimal fertilizing; skip it entirely in fall and winter when growth naturally slows.

## Common varieties and how to tell them apart

- **Golden pothos:** Green leaves with yellow marbling; the most common and most light-tolerant variety.
- **Marble Queen:** Heavily variegated with white and cream patches; needs brighter light to maintain its variegation and grows more slowly than golden pothos.
- **Neon pothos:** Solid, vivid chartreuse-green leaves with no variegation; tolerates lower light well since it has no white/cream sections lacking chlorophyll.
- **Jade pothos:** Solid deep green, very tolerant of low light — a good pick for the darkest rooms in your home.
- **Pearls and Jade:** Smaller leaves with white, green, and cream variegation; generally slower-growing and a bit more light-demanding than golden pothos.

## Common problems and fixes

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Yellow, soft leaves | Overwatering | Let soil dry out more; check roots for rot |
| Brown, crispy leaf edges | Low humidity or fertilizer buildup | Increase humidity; flush soil if recently fertilized heavily |
| Leggy growth with large gaps between leaves | Insufficient light | Move to brighter indirect light or add a [grow light](/blog/best-grow-lights-for-apartments/) |
| Variegation fading to solid green | Not enough light for that variety | Move to a brighter spot; trim solid-green stems to encourage variegated growth |
| Small, sparse leaves | Underfeeding or a pot that's too small | Check pot size and consider a light fertilizing schedule |

## Propagation

Pothos is famously easy to propagate through water rooting: cut just below a node, remove any leaves that would sit underwater, and place in a jar of water in bright, indirect light. Roots typically appear within 1–2 weeks. See our full [propagation guide](/blog/how-to-propagate-houseplants-from-cuttings/) for the complete process, including when to move a rooted cutting into soil.

## Is pothos safe for pets?

No — pothos is toxic to cats and dogs if chewed, due to calcium oxalate crystals that cause oral irritation, drooling, and vomiting. If you have pets prone to chewing plants, keep pothos in a hanging planter or high shelf out of reach, or consider a non-toxic alternative from our [pet-safe plant guide](/blog/pet-safe-houseplants-guide/).
