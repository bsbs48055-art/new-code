20 Pet-Safe Houseplants for Homes With Cats and Dogs

The Leafy Nook Editorial Team April 1, 2026 · 4 min read

In this article

  1. Important note before you rely on any list
  2. 20 pet-safe houseplants
  3. Common houseplants that are actually toxic to pets
  4. Managing toxic plants if you already own them
  5. If your pet does eat part of a toxic plant

If you share your home with a curious cat or a dog that treats every low-hanging leaf as a snack, plant safety isn’t optional — many of the most popular houseplants (pothos, philodendron, and monstera among them) are toxic to pets if chewed or ingested. The good news: there are plenty of genuinely beautiful, easy-care alternatives that are non-toxic.

Important note before you rely on any list

Always verify a specific plant against the ASPCA’s searchable list of toxic and non-toxic plants before purchasing, since common names vary regionally and some plants are sold under misleading labels. This article is a helpful starting point, not a substitute for that verification or for veterinary advice if your pet has already ingested something.

20 pet-safe houseplants

  1. Spider plant (Chlorophytum comosum) — Non-toxic, fast-growing, and actually tolerates being nibbled without harm to the plant or your pet.
  2. Boston fern (Nephrolepis exaltata) — Non-toxic and thrives in the humidity of a bathroom (see our bathroom plants guide).
  3. Calathea (all common varieties) — Non-toxic and visually striking, though somewhat fussier about humidity.
  4. Parlor palm (Chamaedorea elegans) — A non-toxic palm that also tolerates low light well.
  5. Areca palm (Dypsis lutescens) — Non-toxic and a good larger statement plant for pet owners.
  6. Prayer plant (Maranta leuconeura) — Non-toxic, with dramatic leaves that fold up at night.
  7. Ponytail palm (Beaucornea recurvata) — Non-toxic despite its dramatic, spiky appearance.
  8. Peperomia (most varieties) — Compact, non-toxic, and very low-maintenance.
  9. Haworthia — A non-toxic, succulent-like alternative to aloe (which is toxic to pets).
  10. African violet (Saintpaulia) — A non-toxic flowering option for a bright windowsill.
  11. Friendship plant (Pilea involucrata) — Non-toxic and easy to propagate for more pet-safe plants.
  12. Chinese money plant (Pilea peperomioides) — Non-toxic and a popular, easy-care choice.
  13. Bird’s nest fern (Asplenium nidus) — Non-toxic and does well in humid bathrooms.
  14. Rattlesnake plant (Calathea lancifolia) — Non-toxic, part of the broader calathea family.
  15. Baby rubber plant (Peperomia obtusifolia) — Not to be confused with the toxic “rubber plant” (Ficus elastica) — this peperomia is a safe, similar-looking alternative.
  16. Watermelon peperomia — Non-toxic, compact, and distinctively patterned.
  17. Staghorn fern — Non-toxic and can be mounted rather than potted, keeping it further out of a curious pet’s reach.
  18. Money tree (Pachira aquatica) — Non-toxic, despite the unrelated “money plant” name sometimes used for toxic pothos.
  19. Orchids (Phalaenopsis) — Non-toxic and safe even if a curious cat bats at the flowers.
  20. Air plants (Tillandsia) — Non-toxic and easy to keep out of reach since they don’t require soil or a heavy pot.

Common houseplants that are actually toxic to pets

Worth knowing so you can avoid them or keep them strictly out of reach:

  • Pothos (Epipremnum aureum) — toxic to cats and dogs; causes oral irritation, drooling, and vomiting if chewed.
  • Philodendron (most varieties) — similarly toxic due to calcium oxalate crystals.
  • Monstera deliciosa — toxic for the same reason as philodendron.
  • Sago palm — highly toxic to pets, even in small amounts; can be fatal if ingested.
  • Aloe vera — toxic to cats and dogs, though safe topically for humans.
  • Peace lily — toxic to cats and dogs (causes oral irritation), despite being one of the more commonly recommended low-light houseplants for humans.
  • Snake plant — mildly toxic to pets if ingested; usually causes GI upset rather than severe poisoning, but still worth keeping out of reach.
  • Dieffenbachia (“dumb cane”) — toxic and can cause significant oral swelling and irritation.

Managing toxic plants if you already own them

If you already have toxic plants and don’t want to give them up, you can often keep them safely by:

  • Placing them on high shelves, hanging planters, or rooms your pet doesn’t access.
  • Using a pet-deterrent spray (bitter apple spray, widely available at pet stores) on leaves.
  • Providing pet-safe alternatives nearby, like cat grass, so cats specifically have something appropriate to chew.
  • Keeping a close eye on new pets or kittens/puppies, who are typically more likely to chew exploratively than adult, established pets.

If your pet does eat part of a toxic plant

Contact your veterinarian or an animal poison control hotline immediately, and if possible, bring a photo or sample of the plant with you — this speeds up identification and treatment significantly. Don’t wait for symptoms to appear before calling, since some toxins have delayed effects.
