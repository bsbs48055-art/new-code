Indoor Plant Soil, Explained: A Beginner's Guide to What's in the Bag

Grabbing whatever bag says “potting soil” at the store and using it for every plant you own is one of the most common beginner mistakes — and one of the easiest to fix once you understand what soil is actually doing for a plant.

What soil actually needs to do

A good potting mix has three jobs at once:

  1. Hold enough water and nutrients for the plant to access between waterings.
  2. Drain excess water quickly so roots aren’t sitting in standing moisture.
  3. Allow airflow to the roots — remember from our overwatering guide that roots need oxygen, not just water.

Garden soil dug straight from outside fails at all three indoors — it compacts in a pot, drains poorly, and often carries pests or pathogens. That’s why bagged potting mixes exist, and why different plants need different versions of them.

What’s actually in a bag of potting mix

  • Peat moss or coco coir — the water-and-nutrient-holding base. Coco coir is a more sustainable, renewable alternative to peat and rehydrates more easily once dry.
  • Perlite — those small white, popcorn-like specks. They create air pockets and improve drainage.
  • Vermiculite — a mica-based mineral that holds moisture; more common in seed-starting mixes.
  • Pine bark fines/chips — chunky material that improves drainage and airflow, especially important for aroids like monstera and philodendron.
  • Compost or worm castings — organic matter that adds nutrients.
  • Sand (coarse, not play sand) — sometimes added for extra drainage in succulent/cactus mixes.

Matching soil to plant type

Plant type What it needs Simple mix
Tropical foliage (pothos, philodendron, peace lily) Moisture-retentive but airy Standard indoor potting mix as-is, or mix with 20% perlite
Aroids with aerial roots (monstera, philodendron) Extra chunky, very well-draining 40% potting mix, 30% orchid bark, 20% perlite, 10% coco coir
Succulents & cacti Fast draining, doesn’t hold excess water 50% potting mix, 30% coarse sand or pumice, 20% perlite
Ferns, calathea, prayer plants Consistently moist, high organic matter Standard mix with added compost or coco coir, minimal sand
Orchids (Phalaenopsis) Almost no traditional “soil” at all Pure orchid bark mix, no potting soil
Snake plant, ZZ plant Very well-draining, tolerates drying out Succulent/cactus mix, or standard mix with extra perlite

Should you make your own mix or buy pre-made?

Buying a quality pre-made mix labeled for your plant type (e.g., “cactus & succulent mix,” “aroid mix,” “African violet mix”) is perfectly fine and often the easiest option for a small plant collection. Making your own becomes worthwhile once you have many plants, since buying separate bags for every plant type gets expensive — a base bag of potting mix plus bags of perlite and orchid bark lets you custom-blend for whatever you’re repotting.

Signs your current soil isn’t right for the plant

  • Water runs straight through and out the bottom almost instantly → too sandy/chunky for a moisture-loving plant, or the mix has broken down and lost structure.
  • Soil stays soggy for a week or more after watering → too dense or peat-heavy for the plant’s needs, especially common with succulents planted in standard potting soil.
  • Soil has compacted into a hard, water-repellent block → this happens to peat-heavy mixes over time (often 1–2 years) as organic matter breaks down; it’s a sign the plant needs fresh soil, not necessarily a bigger pot.
  • White, crusty buildup on the soil surface → typically mineral salt buildup from fertilizer or hard tap water, not a soil-type issue, but worth flushing the pot with plenty of water next time you water.

A note on reusing old soil

Old potting soil that’s been through a full growing season has usually lost much of its structure and nutrient content, and can also carry pest eggs (like fungus gnat larvae) or fungal spores from a previous plant. When repotting, it’s worth using at least some fresh mix rather than reusing 100% old soil, even if the plant “seems fine” in it — see our repotting guide for the full process.

Do you need to sterilize soil?

For most home growers, no — commercial bagged mixes are generally free of pests and pathogens out of the box. If you’re reusing soil from a plant that had a confirmed pest or fungal problem, it’s safer to discard it rather than try to sterilize and reuse it; the process (baking in an oven) is messy and the smell is genuinely unpleasant in a home kitchen.
