Best Pots for Indoor Plants: A Practical Drainage-First Buying Guide

The Leafy Nook Editorial Team May 27, 2026 · 4 min read

  2. Rule 2: match material to how often you (actually) water

It’s easy to pick a pot based purely on how it looks on a shelf, but the single biggest factor in whether a pot actually works for a plant long-term is drainage — and it’s the thing most decorative pots on the market get wrong. Here’s how to shop for pots that look good and function well.

Rule 1: drainage holes are non-negotiable

We cover this in detail in our pot sizing guide, but it’s worth repeating as the first filter for any purchase: a pot without at least one drainage hole will eventually cause water to pool at the bottom of the soil, leading to root rot regardless of how carefully you water. If a pot you love doesn’t have one, plan to use it as a decorative outer cachepot rather than the direct planting container.

Rule 2: match material to how often you (actually) water

  • Terracotta: Porous and breathable, drying out faster than other materials — good if you tend to overwater, or for drought-tolerant plants like succulents and snake plants. Less ideal for moisture-loving ferns unless you’re prepared to water more frequently.
  • Glazed ceramic: Non-porous, holds moisture longer — better suited to consistent-moisture plants (calathea, ferns) or for people who tend to underwater.
  • Plastic (nursery pots): Lightweight, moisture-retentive, inexpensive, and easy to size up as a plant grows. A safe, practical default for most plants and most people.
  • Metal: Attractive but can conduct heat quickly, potentially overheating roots in direct sun; best used as an outer decorative cachepot rather than direct planting, and check for drainage carefully since many decorative metal pots lack holes.
  • Concrete/cement: Heavy, stable, and slowly porous; a good look for structural plants like snake plants or ZZ plants, though heavier to move once potted, which matters in a small apartment.

Rule 3: consider the saucer situation

A pot with drainage holes needs somewhere for that water to go — either a saucer underneath or a spot where it’s fine for water to land directly (a bathtub, a balcony, a plant-specific tray). For indoor use on furniture or flooring, always pair a draining pot with a properly sized saucer, and empty it after watering rather than letting the plant sit in the runoff.

Self-watering pots: worth it or not?

Self-watering pots use a reservoir at the base and a wicking mechanism to supply consistent moisture over time, which is genuinely useful for:

  • Frequent travelers who can’t check on plants for a week or more at a time.
  • Consistently-moist-loving plants like ferns, calathea, and peace lilies.
  • People who know they tend to underwater and want a buffer.

They’re a poor fit for drought-tolerant plants like succulents, cacti, and snake plants, which specifically need to dry out fully between waterings — a self-watering pot’s constant moisture supply works against what these plants actually need.

Decorative pots without drainage: how to use them safely

Rather than avoiding beautiful drainage-free pots entirely, use the “nursery pot inside a cachepot” method:

  1. Keep the plant in its plastic grow pot (with drainage holes) exactly as it came from the nursery, or repot into a plain plastic pot with holes.
  2. Set that pot inside the decorative pot, which acts purely as a visual cover.
  3. When watering, either lift the inner pot out to water over a sink and let it drain fully before placing it back, or check regularly for any excess water pooling in the bottom of the outer pot and empty it out.

This approach lets you use almost any decorative container you love without risking your plant’s health.

A quick pre-purchase checklist

  • [ ] Does it have at least one drainage hole, or will it be used strictly as a cachepot?
  • [ ] Is the diameter within 1–2 inches of the plant’s current pot (if repotting), not dramatically larger?
  • [ ] Does the material suit how often you realistically water (or forget to)?
  • [ ] Is there a matching or appropriately sized saucer available if it will sit on furniture or flooring?
  • [ ] Can you comfortably lift and move it once it’s filled with damp soil (relevant for larger floor plants)?

Getting the pot right the first time avoids one of the most common and avoidable sources of houseplant problems — and it’s a five-minute check before checkout, not an expensive mistake to fix later.
