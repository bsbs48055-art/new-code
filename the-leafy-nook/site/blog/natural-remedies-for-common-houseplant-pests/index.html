Natural Remedies for the 6 Most Common Houseplant Pests

Finding pests on a houseplant is unsettling, but almost every common indoor pest is treatable at home without resorting to harsh chemical sprays — which is especially good news if you share your space with kids or pets. The key is correct identification first, since the treatment differs by pest.

1. Spider mites

How to spot them: Fine, spider-web-like webbing between leaves and stems, tiny moving specks (often red, tan, or nearly clear) visible on the underside of leaves, and a stippled, dusty-looking discoloration on leaf surfaces. They thrive in dry indoor air.

Treatment:

  • Rinse the plant thoroughly under a lukewarm shower or sink spray to physically knock mites off.
  • Wipe leaves (especially undersides) with a solution of insecticidal soap or a mild dish soap diluted in water (about 1 teaspoon per liter).
  • Increase humidity around the plant, since spider mites multiply fastest in dry air.
  • Isolate the plant from others for at least two weeks and repeat treatment every 5–7 days, since mite eggs hatch in stages.

2. Mealybugs

How to spot them: Small, white, cottony clusters typically found in leaf joints, along stems, and on the undersides of leaves.

Treatment:

  • Dab visible mealybugs directly with a cotton swab dipped in isopropyl alcohol (70%) — this dissolves their protective waxy coating on contact.
  • Follow up with a full-plant spray of insecticidal soap or a neem oil solution, focusing on hidden spots like leaf axils.
  • Repeat every 7–10 days for at least 3–4 rounds, since mealybug eggs are protected and hatch over time.

3. Fungus gnats

How to spot them: Small, dark flies hovering near the soil surface or around the plant when disturbed. Their larvae live in the top layer of consistently moist soil.

Treatment:

  • Let the soil dry out more between waterings — this is both treatment and prevention, since larvae can’t survive in dry soil.
  • Use yellow sticky traps near the soil surface to catch adults and interrupt their breeding cycle.
  • Top-dress the soil with a layer of coarse sand or diatomaceous earth, which makes the surface inhospitable for egg-laying.
  • For a heavier infestation, a diluted hydrogen peroxide soil drench (1 part 3% hydrogen peroxide to 4 parts water) kills larvae on contact without harming most houseplants.

4. Scale insects

How to spot them: Small, round or oval, brown or tan bumps that look like part of the stem or leaf rather than a bug — they don’t move and can be mistaken for a plant feature at first glance. Often accompanied by sticky “honeydew” residue.

Treatment:

  • Scrape or pick off visible scale with a fingernail or soft toothbrush; their hard shell resists sprays until it’s physically dislodged.
  • Follow with a neem oil or insecticidal soap application to catch newly hatched, still-mobile “crawler” stages.
  • Wipe down leaves and stems to remove sticky honeydew residue, which can otherwise attract sooty mold.

5. Aphids

How to spot them: Small, soft-bodied insects (green, black, or brown) clustered on new growth and flower buds, often alongside sticky honeydew residue and sometimes ants (which farm aphids for the honeydew).

Treatment:

  • A strong, direct spray of water can dislodge and remove much of an aphid population immediately.
  • Follow with insecticidal soap or a neem oil spray on remaining aphids, reapplying every 5–7 days.
  • Check new growth frequently, since aphids target the tenderest tissue first.

6. Whiteflies

How to spot them: Tiny, white, moth-like insects that scatter in a small cloud when the plant is disturbed. Often found on leaf undersides.

Treatment:

  • Yellow sticky traps are very effective for catching adults.
  • Vacuum adults off gently in the early morning when they’re less active (yes, this genuinely works for small infestations).
  • Treat remaining nymphs and eggs with insecticidal soap, focusing on the undersides of leaves, repeating weekly.

General pest prevention habits

  • Quarantine new plants for 1–2 weeks before placing them near your existing collection — this is how most infestations actually start.
  • Inspect leaf undersides during your regular watering routine, not just when a plant already looks unwell.
  • Avoid overwatering, since consistently damp soil is what invites fungus gnats specifically.
  • Wipe dust off leaves periodically — dusty leaves are both less able to photosynthesize efficiently and slightly more attractive to some pests.
  • Increase humidity carefully, since it helps plants that like it (reducing spider mite risk) but shouldn’t tip into consistently soggy soil.

When to consider a stronger treatment

If a natural remedy hasn’t visibly improved things after 3–4 consistent treatment rounds over several weeks, or an infestation has spread to multiple plants, a store-bought systemic insecticide labeled safe for houseplants is a reasonable next step — just isolate treated plants from pets during the treatment window as directed on the product label.
