Monstera Care Guide: How to Get Bigger Leaves With More Fenestrations

Monstera deliciosa, with its dramatic split leaves (called fenestrations), has become one of the most recognizable houseplants around — and one of the most common sources of a specific frustration: “why isn’t my monstera developing holes in its leaves?” We’ll cover that along with the full care rundown.

Light requirements

  • Ideal: Bright, indirect light — this is the single biggest factor in whether a monstera develops large, deeply split leaves versus small, solid ones.
  • Tolerated: Medium indirect light, though growth slows and new leaves are smaller with fewer or no fenestrations.
  • Avoid: Direct, intense sun, which can scorch the leaves, and very low light, which stalls growth and fenestration development almost entirely.

Why your monstera’s leaves aren’t splitting

Fenestrations develop more prominently as a monstera matures and gets more light — younger plants and plants in lower light often produce smaller, solid (or minimally split) leaves for a long time before larger, more dramatically fenestrated leaves appear. If your plant is young or grown in medium light, patience and gradually brighter light (not sudden direct sun, which can shock it) are the main fix, more than any product or technique.

Watering

  • Water when the top 2 inches of soil are dry, typically every 7–12 days depending on pot size, light, and season.
  • Monstera prefers to dry out somewhat between waterings but shouldn’t go bone dry for extended periods — it’s a middle-ground plant between the drought-tolerance of a snake plant and the constant-moisture needs of a fern.
  • Yellowing lower leaves with wet soil point to overwatering; see our overwatering guide for the full troubleshooting process.

Soil and support

Monstera does best in a chunky, well-draining mix — a standard potting mix amended with orchid bark and perlite (roughly 30% potting mix, 40% orchid bark, 30% perlite works well) mimics the airy, well-drained conditions of its native tropical forest floor. See our soil guide for more on building this kind of mix.

As monstera matures, it naturally produces aerial roots and benefits from a moss pole or stake to climb, which encourages larger, more mature leaf growth compared to letting it sprawl unsupported. Aerial roots can be gently guided onto the pole and misted occasionally, or left to grow naturally — both approaches are fine.

Fertilizing

Feed with a balanced liquid fertilizer diluted to half strength every 3–4 weeks during spring and summer, when monstera puts out most of its new growth. Reduce to monthly in fall and stop through winter.

Repotting

Monstera is a fairly vigorous grower and often needs repotting every 1–2 years as a young plant, sizing up 2 inches in diameter at a time. Signs it’s time include roots visibly circling the pot or emerging from drainage holes, and the soil drying out unusually fast because there’s more root than soil. See our full repotting guide for the step-by-step process — for a plant this size, having a second person help support the plant while you handle the pot makes the process much easier.

Common problems and fixes

Symptom Likely cause Fix
Yellowing lower leaves, wet soil Overwatering Let soil dry more; check roots
Brown, crispy edges Low humidity or fertilizer buildup Increase humidity; flush soil if over-fertilized
No fenestrations on new leaves Plant is young, or light is too low Increase indirect light gradually; be patient — this can take multiple growth cycles
Small leaves compared to older growth Insufficient light or nutrients Check both light levels and fertilizing schedule
Brown spots with yellow halos Possible fungal or bacterial leaf spot, often from water sitting on leaves Avoid wetting leaves when watering; improve air circulation

Is monstera safe for pets?

No — monstera is toxic to cats and dogs if chewed, due to calcium oxalate crystals that cause oral irritation and gastrointestinal upset. Given how large and floor-level this plant can get, it’s worth placing it somewhere pets can’t easily reach if you share your home with a chewer, or choosing a non-toxic alternative from our pet-safe plant guide.

Propagation

Monstera propagates well from stem cuttings that include at least one node and, ideally, an existing aerial root, which speeds up rooting. Water propagation works but is slower than for pothos, often taking 3–6 weeks to show meaningful root growth — see our propagation guide for the full method.
