How to Propagate Houseplants From Cuttings (A Beginner-Proof Method)

The Leafy Nook Editorial Team March 25, 2026 · 4 min read

In this article

  2. Plants that propagate especially easily in water
  3. Propagating plants that don't do well in water
  4. Common propagation problems
  5. Do cuttings need rooting hormone?

Propagation is one of the most rewarding parts of keeping houseplants — a single healthy pothos or philodendron can realistically produce a dozen new plants over its lifetime, whether for your own collection, as gifts, or to trade with other plant owners. For most common houseplants, the process is far simpler than it sounds.

The easiest method: water propagation

This works especially well for vining and trailing plants with visible nodes along the stem — pothos, philodendron, monstera, and tradescantia are all excellent candidates.

What you’ll need

  • Clean, sharp scissors or pruning shears
  • A glass or jar of room-temperature water
  • A bright, indirect-light spot (not direct sun, which can overheat water and stress the cutting)

Steps

  1. Find a node. This is the small bump or ridge on the stem where a leaf attaches, and it’s where new roots will actually form — cutting anywhere else won’t root.
  2. Cut just below a node, at roughly a 45-degree angle, using clean scissors. Include at least 2–3 nodes and 1–2 leaves per cutting.
  3. Remove any leaves that would sit below the waterline — submerged leaves rot and can cloud the water.
  4. Place the cutting in water so at least one node is submerged, and set it in bright, indirect light.
  5. Change the water every 5–7 days to keep it fresh and oxygenated, which reduces the risk of rot.
  6. Wait for roots. Most common houseplants show the first root growth within 1–3 weeks, though some (like monstera) can take longer.
  7. Pot up once roots are 1–2 inches long. Plant in slightly damp, well-draining soil (see our soil guide) and keep the soil consistently moist for the first couple of weeks while the plant adjusts from water roots to soil roots.

Plants that propagate especially easily in water

  • Pothos — Nearly foolproof; roots often appear within 1–2 weeks.
  • Philodendron (heartleaf and similar vining types) — Very similar process and timeline to pothos.
  • Monstera — Needs a node and, ideally, an existing aerial root on the cutting; roots more slowly (3–6 weeks) but reliably.
  • Tradescantia — Roots extremely fast, often within a week.
  • Chinese money plant (Pilea) — Propagates well both from stem cuttings and from the small “pups” it naturally produces at its base.

Propagating plants that don’t do well in water

Some plants root better going directly into soil or a specific medium rather than sitting in water:

  • Snake plant: Can be propagated via leaf cuttings placed directly in slightly moist, well-draining soil, or by dividing the rhizome (root clump) when repotting.
  • Succulents: Leaf or stem cuttings should be left to callus (dry and seal) for 1–3 days before placing on top of dry, well-draining soil — going straight into wet conditions causes rot.
  • African violets: Leaf cuttings root well directly in a moist, light potting mix or a specialized rooting medium, rather than water.

Common propagation problems

  • Cutting turns mushy or black: Usually rot from water that wasn’t changed often enough, or a leaf submerged below the waterline. Start a fresh cutting with clean water.
  • No root growth after 4+ weeks: Check that the cutting includes an actual node, that it’s getting bright indirect light (not a dim corner), and that water is being changed regularly.
  • Roots grow but the plant struggles after potting in soil: This is common — roots grown in water are structurally different from soil roots. Keep the soil more consistently moist than usual for the first 2 weeks after transplanting to ease the transition, then return to your normal watering routine.

Do cuttings need rooting hormone?

For easy, fast-rooting plants like pothos or philodendron, no — plain water is enough. For slower or trickier plants (some succulents, woody-stemmed plants), a rooting hormone powder or gel applied to the cut end before planting can improve success rates and speed, though it’s optional rather than essential for most common houseplants.

What to do with your new plants

Beyond expanding your own collection, rooted cuttings make genuinely thoughtful, low-cost gifts, and trading cuttings with other plant owners (in person or through local plant-swap groups) is one of the most affordable ways to diversify a collection without buying new plants outright.
