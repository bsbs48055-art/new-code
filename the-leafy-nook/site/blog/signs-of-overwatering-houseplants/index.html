10 Signs You're Overwatering Your Houseplants (And How to Fix It)

Here’s the uncomfortable truth about houseplant care: most “I killed my plant” stories aren’t about neglect. They’re about love — specifically, watering out of guilt or habit far more often than the plant actually needs. Overwatering is quietly the leading cause of houseplant death, and it’s sneaky because the early symptoms look almost identical to a thirsty plant.

Here’s how to actually tell the difference, and what to do about it.

The 10 signs

  1. Yellowing leaves that feel soft or mushy. Underwatered leaves yellow and go crispy/dry. Overwatered leaves yellow and go limp or squishy — that’s the key difference.
  2. Lower and older leaves dropping while the plant looks otherwise “full.” This is the plant’s response to waterlogged roots that can no longer support all its foliage.
  3. Soil that’s still damp a week (or more) after watering. Stick a finger in 2 inches deep — if it’s cool and wet well past when you’d expect it to dry, that’s your answer.
  4. A sour, musty, or swampy smell from the pot. Healthy soil smells earthy. Waterlogged, rotting soil smells distinctly off.
  5. Mushy, dark, or black patches at the base of the stem. This usually indicates stem or crown rot has already begun.
  6. Roots that are brown and mushy instead of white/tan and firm (visible if you gently remove the plant from its pot).
  7. Fungus gnats — small, dark flies hovering around the soil surface. They breed specifically in consistently moist soil.
  8. Edema — small blister-like bumps or corky patches on the underside of leaves, caused by roots absorbing more water than the leaves can transpire.
  9. Mold or a white, fuzzy layer on the soil surface.
  10. A plant that droops even though the soil is clearly wet — often mistaken for “it needs water,” when it’s actually the opposite: damaged roots can no longer take up water even though it’s available.

Overwatering vs. underwatering: a quick comparison

Symptom Overwatered Underwatered
Leaf texture Soft, mushy, sometimes translucent Dry, crispy, curling inward
Soil Wet, heavy, sour smell Dry, pulling away from pot edges
Leaf drop Lower leaves first, still green-ish yellow Older leaves brown and crisp
Stem Can be mushy or blackened at base Firm, but leaves may wilt
Pot weight Heavy Noticeably light

Why overwatering is actually about oxygen, not water

Roots don’t just absorb water — they also need oxygen from air pockets in the soil to function and to fend off rot-causing fungi and bacteria. When soil stays saturated, those air pockets fill with water, roots essentially suffocate, and opportunistic pathogens (particularly Pythium and Phytophthora species) move in. This is root rot, and it’s the actual mechanism behind most “overwatering deaths” — the plant isn’t drowning from too much water in the way you’d picture; its roots are suffocating and then rotting.

How to fix an overwatered plant

  1. Stop watering immediately and let the soil dry out more than you normally would before the next watering.
  2. Move it to brighter, indirect light and better air circulation (a small fan nearby can help soil dry faster) if it isn’t already there.
  3. Unpot and inspect the roots if you see multiple signs above at once. Healthy roots are firm and pale; rotten roots are brown/black, mushy, and may have a foul smell.
  4. Trim away rotten roots with clean, sharp scissors, cutting back to firm, healthy tissue.
  5. Repot into fresh, well-draining soil in a pot with drainage holes — reusing waterlogged soil just reintroduces the problem. See our soil guide for the right mix.
  6. Hold off on fertilizer until you see new, healthy growth; damaged roots can’t process nutrients well and fertilizer salts can add further stress.
  7. Water sparingly going forward, using the finger test from our watering frequency guide rather than a fixed schedule.

Prevention beats recovery

  • Always use pots with drainage holes (see our pot sizing guide).
  • Never let a pot sit in standing water in a saucer or cachepot.
  • Choose a well-draining soil mix appropriate to the plant.
  • Water based on soil moisture, not a fixed calendar — and water less in fall/winter when growth naturally slows.
  • When unsure, wait. A slightly thirsty plant recovers in hours; a plant with rotted roots can take weeks, if it recovers at all.

Can an overwatered plant come back?

Often, yes — especially if you catch it in the early stages (yellowing leaves, damp soil) rather than the late stages (widespread mushy stems, foul smell, collapsed growth). The earlier you intervene with the steps above, the better the odds. For plants that are already badly wilted or rotting throughout, see our companion piece on reviving a dying houseplant for a full triage process.
